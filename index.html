Eulogy for my father

Remarks for my father, Dr. Donald Barry Nelson, delivered November 13, 2026, at a memorial gathering at the Harvard School of Dental Medicine, where he taught orthodontics.

Eulogy Documents

Thank you to Harvard, to Mo, to all of you for making the effort to be here to celebrate my dad, and to Sercan, the Head of Orthodontics here, who told me the focus today would be academic.

So I thought it fitting to begin with my father’s progress report from fifth grade at the Carteret School.

February 17, 1956

Dear Mr. and Mrs. Nelson:

I am writing to give you a brief report on Donald’s progress for the first semester.

He is an above-average student … Mr. George says, “He is sometimes too noisy to be pleasant, but a refreshing personality to have around.”

I feel that Donald is developing a well-rounded personality. It is a pleasure to have him at Carteret.

Sincerely yours,
Thompson D. Grant
Director

For those of you wondering whether Mr. George had it right, whether my father was, in fact, too noisy to be pleasant, may I remind you that he played the drums.

And when we were growing up, he didn’t keep them in the garage. He set them up in the middle of our living room… which I recall was… refreshing.

✦

I also have here his transcript from the University of Pennsylvania School of Dental Medicine.

First year. Biochemistry. Grade: 73.

Which is not the grade you imagine of a future Harvard professor. It is the grade of a dental student with a rapidly improving tennis game.

But the transcript tells a story: a young man who started slowly but by his final year was fourth in a class of 130.

My father would want me to point out that none of the three people ahead of him ever amounted to much. And certainly none of them became president of the Massachusetts Association of Orthodontists.

That honor went instead to a boy once judged too noisy to be pleasant.

One more number. 92. His highest grade: orthodontics.

It’s a profession built on the idea that a small force, applied patiently, can change something that once seemed permanent.

My father believed that about teeth. He believed it about students.

He loved practicing orthodontics. Teaching it. Talking about it.

Many of you were participants in a conversation he never wanted to end.

I know this because I spent a significant part of my childhood waiting for those conversations to end.

What I wouldn’t give to wait just one more time.

✦

My father was the most optimistic person I’ve ever known.

So much optimism that it made him worry.

Which sounds contradictory, until you remember: we’re Jews.

We call ourselves the chosen people, without fully appreciating that what we’ve been chosen for is to worry.

So, perhaps unsurprisingly, he once told me, “I’m worried.”

I said, “What’s up?”

He said, “I think I need to work about another 40 years to support my lifestyle.”

The optimistic part? He was 77.

Most men at 77 are thinking about downsizing. A condo closer to the grandchildren. A pottery hobby.

Not him. Somewhere around 117, he was prepared to reassess.

Which may explain the grade of 73 in biochemistry.

But confidence in the future and confidence in yourself are two different things.

He could be proud of his work. His students. His profession.

Pride in himself was harder.

His work gave him something the rest of life didn’t always provide. A world that made sense, where a problem could be identified, studied, brought into alignment.

And he never had to worry that those 28 teeth would abandon him.

What protects us and what makes us happy are not always the same thing.

I’m grateful that my father, at least in his work, found both.

✦

He was equal parts charisma, charm, complication, and fourth meals eaten straight from the refrigerator. A beautiful guy who struggled to see the beauty in himself. Which is why he struggled to be alone.

I loved him. Not because I didn’t know his flaws. Because I knew them well.

As his son, I’ve inherited several. Including a certain instinct to deflect accountability.

If he were here, he’d say, “I don’t think you get that from me.”

He was confident, and he was insecure. He was optimistic, and he worried. He made a career of creating order. And yet you should have seen the inside of his car.

Which was less a car and more a storage unit with a steering wheel. There were things living in that car with their own retirement plans. My father wasn’t one of them.

Because he loved what he did, and he gave himself to it completely. And he wanted to share it, with his colleagues, his students, his family.

I came home one time for Father’s Day and asked what he wanted to do, and he said, “I want to give you Invisalign. Your lowers are shifting.”

It was a sign of his love that I had braces for an entire Bush administration and a year of Clinton.

I was, after all, a walking advertisement for the practice.

And the one patient who couldn’t get a second opinion.

✦

Teaching, however, was a pure form of love for him.

My father had two children with my mother, but with orthodontics he had hundreds.

He got very attached to you all. When I was in college, I got time that sons rarely get with their fathers: I lived with him while I was in school in Cambridge. I saw firsthand how he tried to turn his home into the Harvard orphanage for orthodontic residents. With some success, I might add. He’d invite you to dinner, then over to the house, then try to make you a partner in his practice. Hey, why not?

He could see potential in you that you couldn’t see in yourself. He could encourage it. And he could take enormous pride in what it became.

Teaching fed his ego, sure, but also kept it in check. He waited until 78 to write a lecture about learning from mistakes. It was the first time he admitted he’d ever made one.

I think the veneer of perfection was his way of trying to earn something he was never sure he already had.

✦

Which brings me back to the Carteret School, and to my father’s progress report from the following year.

February 18th, 1957

Dear Mr. and Mrs. Nelson:

It is a pleasure to write you about Donald as he has been doing excellent work as his report card so clearly indicates.

His teachers report that he is dependable, co-operative, conscientious, and in addition a very bright boy.

…

We are delighted to have Donald with us and you should be very proud of him. He is the type of boy who will go far and do well in College.

Sincerely,
Carteret School
George Douglas Hofe, President

I want to tell that little boy:

They say you’ll go far. And you will. And you’ll spend a good part of the journey wondering whether you’re worthy of the good things said about you.

You are.

And one day, you will be surrounded not by what you accumulated but by what you gave away. It’s here, in rows, in a room you can still fill, even in your absence.

Nearly seventy years later.

I wish he could see this room.

Not because he needed another accomplishment.

But because I wish he could see what all of you saw in him.

What his teachers saw.

What I saw.

His parents were told they should be proud of him.

I’m not sure he ever got there himself.

This past July, in the ER, my father, already frail and ill, turned to me and said, “Do you forgive me?” I told him there was nothing to forgive. He began to cry, and I held his head, and I told him, over and over: You are so so so loved. And I am so so so proud of you.

So while we’re here, tell each other what you see in one another. Your friends. Children. Partners. Parents.

Don’t leave it to a letter.

And in honor of my dad, be pleasant, but don’t forget to be a little noisy, too.

Thank you.

— Peter Nelson
